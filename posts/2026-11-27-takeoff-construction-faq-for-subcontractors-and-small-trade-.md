# Takeoff Construction FAQ for Subcontractors and Small Trade Teams

Questions about takeoff construction come up constantly from subcontractors who are new to estimating, switching from paper, or considering AI-assisted tools. This FAQ answers the most common ones in plain language.

## The basics

### What is a takeoff in construction?

A takeoff is the process of extracting quantities from construction drawings. That means counting discrete items, such as outlets, fixtures or sprinkler heads, and measuring lengths and areas. Those quantities are the foundation for pricing the work.

### How is a takeoff different from an estimate?

The takeoff gives you quantities. The estimate turns those quantities into costs by applying rates, then adds allowances and margin. The quote is what you send to the client, with inclusions, exclusions and assumptions.

### Why does takeoff accuracy matter so much for subcontractors?

Every price you send is built on the quantities. If a count is low, you underprice the work. If it is high, you may lose the bid. For small trade teams with thin margins, small counting errors can decide whether a job pays.

## Doing the work

### Where should I start on a new drawing set?

Start with the title blocks to confirm you have the latest revision, then read the legend for the symbols your trade prices. Note any symbols that look alike. Only then start counting.

### What are the most common takeoff mistakes?

Counting from an outdated revision, misreading look-alike symbols, missing scope that appears only in notes or schedules, double counting at match lines between sheets, and errors when copying numbers into a pricing spreadsheet or quote.

### Should I use drawing markup during a takeoff?

Yes. Marking up the drawings helps you track what has been counted and flag questions for the general contractor. It also gives you a visual record if someone later asks how you arrived at a quantity.

## AI and software

### What are AI-generated counts?

They are counts proposed by software that detects symbols on drawings. They speed up the first pass on repeated items, which is where manual counting is slowest.

### Can I send AI counts straight into a quote?

You should not. Treat AI-generated counts as a first draft and review them: check totals for sense, inspect the densest sheet closely, and watch for look-alike symbols and scope boundaries. The responsibility for the final number stays with you.

### Why does it matter whether takeoff and pricing are in the same tool?

Because every time numbers move between tools, they can be mistyped, misaligned or left out of date. Keeping the takeoff, rates and quote in one place removes most of those handoffs.

## Team and process

### Who should review a takeoff?

Ideally someone other than the person who did the count. Even in a two-person team, swapping roles for the review catches mistakes the original counter would read straight past.

### How do I handle revised drawings?

Log the revision, confirm the latest version of every sheet, find what changed, update only the affected counts and re-price them. Then make sure your quote states which revision you priced.

### How can a small team speed up bids without cutting corners?

Reduce copying between tools, let AI handle the first pass on repeated items, and keep a short written review routine that every bid follows. Speed comes from removing tedious steps, not from skipping checks.
