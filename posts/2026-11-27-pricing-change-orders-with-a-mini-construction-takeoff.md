# Pricing Change Orders With a Mini Construction Takeoff

Once a job is won, the drawings rarely stay still. A room gets reconfigured, a client adds a few items, a design detail changes. Each of those needs a price, and small trade teams often produce it in a hurry: a quick look, a number on the back of an envelope, a text to the site manager. That's how change orders get underpriced and margin leaks away over a project.

The fix is to treat every change order as a mini construction takeoff: a short, structured count of exactly what changed, priced with the same discipline as the original bid.

## Step 1: Define the change precisely

Before counting, pin down what the change actually is. Get it in writing if you can: a revised sheet, a marked-up drawing, an email describing it. Write one sentence describing the change in your own words and confirm it with whoever requested it.

Vague changes produce arguments later. Adding some extra outlets in the meeting rooms is a different job from adding six outlets per meeting room on level 2 as shown on a specific revised sheet.

## Step 2: Count three things, not one

A change isn't just new items. Count:

- **Added:** new items to supply and install.
- **Removed:** items in the original scope no longer needed, which may or may not be credited, especially if already bought.
- **Modified:** items that stay but move, change type or need rework.

Modified items are the ones most often forgotten. Moving something already installed can take longer than installing it new.

## Step 3: Price the disruption, not just the items

The original bid assumed a sequence. A change can break it. Think about:

- Return visits to areas already finished.
- Work in areas other trades have since closed up.
- Material already ordered that can't be returned.
- Programme impact if the change delays your other work.

These costs are real but invisible in a count. List them as separate lines so the client can see them.

## Step 4: Use the same rates as the original quote

Consistency builds trust. If the original quote showed rates per item, use the same ones for the change unless conditions are genuinely different, and if they are, explain why. A change priced with unexplained higher rates invites a dispute.

## Step 5: Reference the original

Every change order should say which drawing or instruction it responds to and how it relates to the original scope. That makes the final account much easier to agree.

## Keeping change orders connected to the original job

```

Requested by / date:
Instruction or drawing reference:
Description (one sentence):

Added:      item | qty | rate | total
Removed:    item | qty | credit (if any)
Modified:   item | qty | rate | total
Disruption: description | cost

Total:
Programme impact:
Assumptions:
Valid until:
```

## Habits that protect margin

- **Never agree a price verbally on site.** Say you'll send it the same day, and do.
- **Price every change, even small ones.** Small unpriced changes add up.
- **Keep a running log.** A list of all changes, their status and value makes the final account straightforward.
